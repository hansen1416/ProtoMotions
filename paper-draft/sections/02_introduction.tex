\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}
A physics-based motion controller should reproduce an action across bodies
with different proportions and dynamics. Changing limb lengths, collision
geometry, and mass distribution changes both a motion's spatial realization
and the actuation needed to follow it. Kinematic retargeting alone is not
sufficient: the reference must correspond to the simulated body, and a
feedback policy must track it.

Large motion resources, shape-conditioned generation, and scalable imitation
provide the building blocks~\cite{amass,humanml3d,humos,phc,protomotions}.
Their combination raises a practical question: can one controller track a
large collection of paired, morphology-specific motions across many bodies,
and how reliably does it transfer to configurations absent from training?
We address this through system construction and empirical evaluation.
Morphology conditioning and temporal attention are established techniques;
the contribution is a consistent paired-motion workflow at this scale and
an evaluation distinguishing motion exposure from body exposure.

HUMOS generates references for 128 configurations; simulation assets use
the same SMPL parameters. Clip/body identities are preserved through
conversion, refinement, training, and replay. Refinement reduces contact
artifacts with explicit fidelity checks. A shared temporal slot/type-attention
policy trains directly across bodies, with morphology in actor and critic
and a streamed pool limiting resident memory. HumanML3D captions support
descriptive analysis but are not policy inputs.

We make three contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
\item A paired corpus construction linking source identities, shape-specific
references, matching assets, and deterministic clip-grouped splits across
20,951 clips and 128 body configurations.
\item A shared imitation system trained on 18,855 clips across all 128
bodies, combining temporal observations, morphology inputs, reference
processing, and memory-bounded motion streaming.
\item An empirical assessment separating held-out tracking, trained-body
coverage, and unseen-body transfer, with repeated-seed design comparisons
and an explicit account of residual failures and evidence limits.
\end{enumerate}

The original checkpoint achieves 99.14\% success on the independent
1,048-clip test split, with one selected trained body per clip. On a separate
1,024-clip panel over 32 unseen bodies, overall success is 91.64\%: 28 bodies
reach 99.74\% collectively, while four transfer poorly. The full-population
score is central to the generalization claim. These findings support shared
control over substantial motion and body variation, but also reveal limits
that aggregate trained-body scores conceal. Fixed all-body held-out coverage
and causal tests of morphology inputs are needed for stronger claims.
