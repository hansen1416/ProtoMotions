\section{Results}\label{sec:results}
We report held-out tracking, transfer to unseen bodies, and the design
comparisons, and use per-body failures to delimit the claims.

\subsection{Tracking across motions and bodies}\label{sec:tracking}

\paragraph{Held-out motions on selected trained bodies.}\label{sec:test_results}
The epoch-28,170 checkpoint was re-evaluated offline with a standalone
evaluator, once on the validation split and once on the clip-disjoint test
split, under the protocol of Section~\ref{sec:experiments}: one selected body per
clip (shape panel index 110), the exact asset for each reference, and mean
actions. All 1,048 clips of each split last about 6.6\,s (198 evaluated
frames), so none reaches the 600-step cap. The test manifest is loaded only by
this evaluator, which first verifies its hash, clip count, and disjointness from
train and validation; the test split was evaluated once and not used to select
the checkpoint.

Validation and test scores are similar (1,032 and 1,039 of 1,048 successes),
and no significance claim is made from their difference. Continuous pose errors
and jerk complement the permissive 0.5\,m threshold. The Wilson
intervals~\cite{wilson1927} are nominal: they condition on the checkpoint and
selected body panel and ignore dependence between related recordings or
mirrored clips, training variability, and uncertainty across body selections.

\paragraph{Coverage of trained bodies.}
Replaying 150 training clips on all 128 trained bodies (19,200 pairs) reaches
99.29\% success with the same checkpoint (Table~\ref{tab:unseen_v2}), which
establishes broad tracking on that subset. All-body consistency on held-out
motions remains unmeasured: it would need a held-out-motion panel crossed with
all 128 bodies. Figure~\ref{fig:trained_multibody_demo} shows one motion executed
across the 128 trained bodies; it illustrates execution and does not quantify
tracking, contact quality, or transfer.

\paragraph{Residual failures.}\label{sec:failures}
Nine test and 16 validation pairs fail the threshold. Their captions suggest
ground transitions (six test, five validation failures) and whole-body
rotations (two test, four validation), and also mention turning, side-stepping,
an abrupt stop, and a hand-held sparring stick absent from simulation. These
describe the failed sample rather than mechanical causes or class-specific
failure rates; diagnosis would need synchronized rollouts and first-crossing
traces, including successful variants of the same clips.

\subsection{Transfer to unseen bodies}\label{sec:unseen_morphology}

\paragraph{High average transfer inside and outside the training range.}
Crossing 233 training-split clips with all 128 bodies in each unseen panel gives
29,824 pairs per panel, and Table~\ref{tab:unseen_v2} includes every body and
every failed pair. The original checkpoint reaches 97.28\% inside-range and
97.15\% outside-range success. The trained-body control reaches 99.29\%, but uses
different clips and a different refinement run, so the gap of roughly two
percentage points is descriptive, not a matched estimate of the effect of
changing bodies. Deltas are paired within each dataset, intervals resample clips
with the body panel fixed, and the rerun repeats the original checkpoint, not its
training.

\paragraph{Failures concentrate in individual bodies.}
High averages coexist with severe body-specific failures. Inside body
\texttt{male\_669a0fb3} succeeds on 3.0\% of clips and outside body
\texttt{male\_1c099619} on 13.3\%; another inside body, \texttt{male\_ec3bb7f8},
reaches only 19.3\% despite an in-range mass of 42.7\,kg and height of 1.41\,m.
Figure~\ref{fig:unseen_v2_shapes} shows all 128 bodies of each panel, sorted
independently by original-checkpoint success on the same 233 known clips (the
ranking does not pair inside and outside bodies), and
the Supplementary Material reports every body. Lying outside the training
coefficient box does not explain these failures: success does not decline
monotonically across the four outside coefficient tiers (99.03, 97.76, 95.33,
and 96.47\%). Bodies outside the trained height range perform well on average
(97.91\% outside; 96.42\% inside), while the tallest in-range bin is weaker
(90.53\% outside; 88.75\% inside). The eight heaviest outside bodies, above
144.4\,kg, average 79.02\%, but small overlapping property groups and paired
male/female templates are not independent samples of physical-property space, so
coefficient extremity, mass, and height give no simple ordering of transfer
difficulty (tier and mass tables in Appendix~\ref{app:unseen_tables}). All failed
rows have zero recorded termination flags; because flags depend on the replay
configuration rather than an independent fall detector, we treat these as
threshold failures pending synchronized rollouts and contact and root-height
traces.

\paragraph{Checkpoint sensitivity.}
The later full-data checkpoint (epoch 34,000) differs from the original by
$-0.37$ pp inside and $+0.42$ pp outside (Table~\ref{tab:unseen_v2}); both exceed
the identical-checkpoint rerun change, but duration and motion coverage changed
together, so the change cannot be attributed to either, and ordinary checkpoint
variation remains unmeasured. The outside gain is concentrated in the ten
bodies beyond the trained mass range ($+7.68$ pp, 95\% CI [$+6.39$, $+9.01$])
and the outermost coefficient tier ($+1.39$ pp [$+1.07$, $+1.72$]), while mean
position error rises by 2.4, 5.0, and 3.1\,mm in the outside, inside, and
control panels, so threshold success and continuous tracking move differently.
Three inside and six outside bodies regress by more than 2 pp with intervals
excluding zero, by up to 36.5 pp, and five outside bodies improve by more than
2 pp, including \texttt{male\_1c099619} (13.3\% to 71.2\%)
(Appendix~\ref{app:unseen_tables}); neither improved extremes nor a higher
outside average justify calling the later checkpoint uniformly better. On the
23-clip diagnostic subset, which the later checkpoint trained on, inside success
is 97.08\% versus 96.50\% and outside 96.81\% versus 96.91\% for the original and
later checkpoints; these values are excluded from the headline. Severe failures
remain unresolved: per-asset reference feasibility and synchronized contact,
root-height, and actuation traces have not been audited, so reference,
controller, and asset mechanisms cannot be distinguished.

\subsection{Design comparisons}\label{sec:design}

We compare four architectures: A, temporal MLP; B, temporal attention; C,
attention with slot/type embeddings; and D, C with actor-only adaLN-Zero. All
retain morphology inputs in actor and critic. Refined-reference runs use three
seeds (0, 1, 2) and a matched 6,000-epoch budget on the development subset, and
each run contributes one summary, the mean of its last eight evaluations
(epochs 4,600--6,000); means and sample standard deviations are computed across
the three run summaries, and within-run evaluations are not replicates. Seed-1/2
values were checked against local TensorBoard records, whereas the original logs
of the seed-0 values were not independently available. The full-scale model
predates these reruns, which reassess its design rather than establish
prospective selection. Cases E--H repeat A--D with unrefined references and one
seed each (Appendix~\ref{app:history}).

Position and rotation errors are across-seed means, and parameters are actor
and critic totals. Attention variants reach 97.2--97.9\% mean success against
95.1\% for the temporal MLP, with lower position error (0.083--0.090 versus
0.118\,m) and much smaller seed variability (standard deviation 0.08--0.34
versus 2.07 points), although the MLP has the most parameters, roughly
26--29\% more than the attention variants. Among the attention variants, C has
the highest mean success and D the lowest position and rotation errors. A has
the lowest mean jerk, but jerk differences between architectures are
comparable to the seed-to-seed standard deviations (643--887), so the apparent
factor-of-two jerk advantage in seed 0 is not a consistent architecture
effect. Three seeds do not establish statistical superiority, and we retain C as
a practical compromise.

\paragraph{Reference refinement.}\label{sec:refinement_results}
A paired offline audit of all 19,200 development references (150 clips
$\times$ 128 bodies; no simulation) compares artifact and fidelity measures
before and after refinement (Table~\ref{tab:refine_audit}). Artifact rows give
the corpus mean before and after and the fraction of motions that improve;
fidelity rows give the corpus mean refined-versus-unrefined difference.
Refinement lowers stance-foot speed during detected contact from 0.055 to
0.038\,m/s, with 96\% of references improving, and below-floor frames from
39.6\% to 3.3\% (only 45\% of references improve on the latter because many
were already grounded). Measured motion features are largely preserved: the mean
per-body rotation change is $0.12^\circ$, root horizontal displacement is
0.008\,m, and the cross-shape variance of knee-flexion range is retained at a
ratio of 0.94. The cost is higher reference jerk (the raw mean third difference
of body positions, not the windowed evaluator score), from 45.2 to
60.7\,m/s$^3$, with 79\% of references worsening; temporal root and clearance
corrections are a candidate explanation, but their contribution to policy
smoothness is not isolated. Refined and unrefined policies (cases A--D versus
E--H) track different targets, so their comparison describes different tasks
and does not establish a causal benefit of refinement, which would need a
common-reference checkpoint comparison.

\paragraph{Scope of the morphology-conditioning evidence.}
Every architecture receives morphology inputs in actor and critic. The adaLN-Zero
variant changes how the actor uses them but does not test their removal, and
body-dependent state and reference targets also expose morphology. Successful
transfer therefore establishes a capability of the whole system, not that
explicit coefficients cause it. Attributing it to those inputs needs a matched
training run without them, with the same references, body panel, budget, and
seeds; inference-time masking or shuffling would introduce distribution shift
and cannot replace that comparison.

\subsection{Exploratory physical analysis}\label{sec:physics}
As a secondary question, we ask whether cross-body variability in control demand
differs between dynamic and quasi-static motions. The original checkpoint is
replayed over the 150 development clips and all 128 trained bodies (19,200
pairs, zero recorded failed episodes under this analysis's own diagnostic).
Keyword matching on the inherited captions assigns running, jumping, and
kicking to a dynamic group (25 clips) and standing and sitting to a
quasi-static group (37 clips); the other 88 clips are excluded, and the labels
have not been manually validated. After subtracting each clip's across-body
mean, Table~\ref{tab:physical_analysis} reports the standard deviation of the
pooled residuals, with 95\% intervals that resample clips as clusters (10,000
resamples). Torque, power, and jerk describe the simulated controller, not
calibrated human biomechanics.
